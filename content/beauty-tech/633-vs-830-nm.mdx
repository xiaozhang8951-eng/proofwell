## The Short Answer

**633 nm is visible red light; 830 nm is near-infrared light.** The numbers describe wavelength in nanometers. They do not describe treatment strength, a guaranteed tissue depth or a quality ranking.

At these wavelengths, light interacts with skin through reflection, absorption and scattering. Those processes depend on both the wavelength and the tissue. A comparison of transmission through a particular skin sample is not a clinical comparison of two masks.<Citation number={6} />

When choosing a device, ask how much light reaches the worn treatment position, how evenly it is distributed, how exposure is timed and what human evidence applies to that model. The more useful question is not simply “which wavelength is bigger?”

## What Is 633 nm Red Light?

633 nm lies in the visible red part of the spectrum. Unlike near-infrared, it can be seen by the human eye. Its visible appearance does not tell you the irradiance received by the skin.

Red light is discussed in skin-related photobiomodulation, or PBM: the investigation of biological responses to certain light exposures. Clinical papers and reviews address particular light sources, protocols and outcomes; the label *633 nm* alone does not carry their findings over to a consumer mask.<Citation number={1} /><Citation number={5} />

When red light reaches skin, some is reflected at the surface. Absorbed light transfers energy to tissue constituents; scattering changes its direction. The remaining light field is distributed through tissue rather than deposited at a single marked layer. Which interactions matter depends on the tissue and exposure conditions.<Citation number={6} />

Proposed cellular mechanisms provide a reason to investigate PBM. They do not establish that every red-light device increases collagen, improves elasticity or produces a visible change. For the broader mechanism discussion, read [How Do LED Face Masks Work?](/science/how-led-masks-work).

## What Is 830 nm Near-Infrared Light?

830 nm lies beyond visible red, in the near-infrared region. **It is invisible to the human eye.** A mask's visible glow therefore cannot establish whether its NIR emitters are operating or how much optical power they deliver. Use the exact model's indicators and instructions rather than judging its output by brightness.

Compared with 633 nm, 830 nm encounters a different combination of absorption and scattering in a given tissue. Red-to-NIR transmission is often discussed in terms of an optical window, but this is not a universal rule that each longer wavelength reaches deeper or works better. Measurements depend on the wavelength, sample and method.<Citation number={6} />

Relevant variables include pigmentation, tissue thickness, blood content, water content, scattering structures and source geometry. In-vivo skin, an excised sample and a simplified model are not interchangeable measurement settings.

Our [Red vs Near-Infrared Light](/science/red-vs-near-infrared) guide addresses the wider spectral comparison. The distinction here is narrower: two particular nominal wavelengths used in LED specifications.

There is no single universal penetration depth for either wavelength. Light does not travel through skin as a straight beam that stops exactly at the epidermis, dermis or underlying tissue. Absorption and repeated scattering shape a continuously changing distribution.<Citation number={6} />

“Penetration depth” also needs a definition: a measurement may refer to a specified fall in light intensity, transmission through a sample or a modelled light distribution. Different definitions cannot be compared as though they describe the same endpoint.

Treat claims such as “633 nm reaches exactly this layer,” “830 nm goes twice as deep” or “deeper means better” cautiously unless the experimental conditions, definition and measurement method are provided. ## Does Deeper Penetration Mean Better Results?

No. Optical transmission, biological interaction and a clinically meaningful outcome are different questions. A useful target may be relatively superficial; light reaching a deeper region is not automatically an advantage.

Consider two **hypothetical** devices: one delivers 633 nm at a higher irradiance at the skin, while the other delivers 830 nm at a lower irradiance. Wavelength alone cannot tell you which produces more exposure at a particular target. The spectral output, spatial distribution and tissue conditions are also needed. This example describes no real product and assigns neither wavelength a superior result.

Even equal radiant exposure does not establish equivalent biological effects. A device-level comparison needs relevant human studies, an appropriate comparator, the delivered protocol and the measured outcome. See [Red Light Therapy for Skin](/science/red-light-therapy-skin) for the separate clinical-evidence question.

## Can a Mask Combine 633 nm and 830 nm?

A device can provide both spectral bands. That is a design choice, not proof that every user needs both or that a combination outperforms either wavelength alone.

The reference list includes reports whose titles describe combined 633/830 nm LED treatment, a comparison of treatment settings and a home-use study at **637/854 nm**. These are different questions and systems; a study at nearby wavelengths is not an exact test of every 633/830 nm mask.<Citation number={1} /><Citation number={2} /><Citation number={3} /><Citation number={4} />

To assess a combination claim, check whether the study actually compared that combination against its individual wavelengths at interpretable exposures. A change from baseline alone does not establish superiority over sham treatment. Results from clinical equipment should not automatically be transferred to home-use masks with similar branding or nominal wavelengths.

## What Matters Besides Wavelength?

### Irradiance at the treatment position

Irradiance is optical power per unit area, commonly expressed in mW/cm². Ask where it was measured: output at an LED surface is not automatically the irradiance at the skin when the mask is worn. Electrical power consumption and LED count are not substitutes for an optical measurement.

### Radiant exposure, often called fluence

Device literature often uses *fluence* or *energy density* for energy delivered per unit area. For this surface calculation, the more precise quantity is radiant exposure. It is not the same as energy absorbed by a biological target.

**Mathematical example, not a treatment recommendation:** 10 mW/cm² × 600 seconds = **6 J/cm²**, because 10 mW/cm² = 0.010 W/cm² and 0.010 W/cm² × 600 s = 6 J/cm².

These are hypothetical values, not a tested mask's specifications or an effective or safe dose. Pulsing and time-varying output require an appropriate average or time integral. For the unit distinction, see [Irradiance vs Fluence](/science/irradiance-vs-fluence).

### Session timing, fit and light distribution

Duration and treatment frequency describe different parts of a protocol. Increasing time does not independently establish a larger benefit; follow the exact device's instructions rather than extending sessions on that assumption.

Fit, source-to-skin distance, facial contours and illumination uniformity influence the exposure pattern. A maximum measured at one point does not tell you the range across the face. For multi-wavelength devices, ask whether output is reported separately for each band or only as an aggregate.

## Why 630, 633, 660 or Other NIR Wavelengths?

630, 633 and 660 nm are different positions within visible red; 810, 830, 850 and 854 nm are different positions within near-infrared. Their labels are not interchangeable protocols.

A real LED emits across a spectral band, and changes in wavelength can alter absorption and scattering. That does not let you infer a clinically important difference from a few nanometers alone. Check the spectral bandwidth, delivered exposure and evidence for the exact device rather than assuming either that all red light is identical or that one nominal number is uniquely effective.<Citation number={6} />

## Safety and Practical Use

Read the exact model's instructions for duration, frequency, positioning and eye protection. Do not assume invisible NIR is safe for the eyes or use visible brightness as a safety test.

If you have an eye condition, photosensitivity disorder, use medicines associated with photosensitivity or have another relevant medical circumstance, seek appropriate professional advice about suitability. Stop use and obtain advice for pain, persistent irritation or visual symptoms. These are precautionary instructions, not an individualized assessment or a universal contraindication list.

Verify any regulatory claim against the exact model and intended use. A regulatory badge is not a clinical ranking of all devices with the same wavelength. This explainer verifies no specific device clearance or approval, and it does not replace prescribed care.

## Choosing Between the Numbers

Neither wavelength is a universal winner. 633 nm is visible red and 830 nm is NIR, with different optical interactions in a specified tissue. Greater transmission, more emitters or a second wavelength does not independently establish a better cosmetic outcome.

The meaningful questions are whether the output is documented at the skin, the exposure is interpretable, the instructions are clear and relevant human evidence supports the intended use. A wavelength is the beginning of that assessment, not its conclusion.
