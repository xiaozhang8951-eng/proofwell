## What Is Irradiance?

**Irradiance is the radiant power incident on a surface per unit area.** Common units are mW/cm², or milliwatts per square centimetre, and W/cm², or watts per square centimetre.

Think of it as the rate at which light energy reaches a given area. For a face mask, the useful quantity is irradiance at a specified skin-facing treatment plane under documented operating conditions—not just a number without a measurement location.<Citation number={1} />

Several specifications are sometimes presented as though they were interchangeable:

- Electrical power consumed by the device.
- Total optical output power.
- Optical power emitted by one LED.
- Irradiance at the mask or emitter surface.
- Irradiance at the actual treatment plane when worn.

These quantities are not equivalent. More electrical power does not necessarily mean more optical power reaching skin. Total LED output does not tell you how much reaches each square centimetre of the face.

**The measurement location matters.** A flexible or curved mask can expose the forehead, cheeks, nose and chin differently because of its LED arrangement, fit, angle and source-to-skin distance. A single maximum reading may conceal substantial spatial variation.

## What Is Fluence?

Device specifications and PBM literature often use *fluence* for **surface radiant exposure**: incident radiant energy per unit area accumulated over an exposure period, expressed in J/cm², or joules per square centimetre. That is the quantity calculated here.

Terminology needs care. This surface measurement does not describe the complete light field inside tissue, nor the energy absorbed by a particular cell or layer. Calling it an optical “dose” does not turn it into a validated clinical treatment.

For continuous, constant irradiance at a stated measurement plane:

**Radiant exposure (J/cm²) = Irradiance (W/cm²) × Time (seconds)**

If irradiance is given in milliwatts rather than watts:

**Radiant exposure (J/cm²) = Irradiance (mW/cm²) × Time (seconds) ÷ 1,000**

Unlike irradiance, radiant exposure accumulates over time. For time-varying irradiance, the general relationship is its time integral. A fluence number alone therefore does not tell you how quickly or uniformly the light was delivered.

### Two hypothetical exposures

- **Condition A:** 10 mW/cm² × 600 seconds ÷ 1,000 = **6 J/cm²**.
- **Condition B:** 5 mW/cm² × 1,200 seconds ÷ 1,000 = **6 J/cm²**.

Both calculate to the same incident radiant exposure, using different rates and durations. These are **mathematical examples only**, not experimentally validated mask parameters or recommended treatment protocols.

## Why Equal Fluence Does Not Guarantee the Same Effect

Equal accumulated energy per area does not fully describe an exposure, so it is not proof of an equivalent biological or clinical effect. Other relevant variables include:

- Irradiance and exposure duration separately.
- Wavelength and spectral bandwidth.
- Pulse frequency, duty cycle and peak versus average output.
- Tissue optical properties and the treatment location.
- Light-source geometry and spatial distribution.
- Repeated exposures and intervals between sessions.

PBM reviews discuss exposure-dependent responses, including biphasic patterns in particular experimental systems. A response observed at one exposure does not guarantee that additional exposure gives a greater benefit. This is not a universal effective-dose curve or a reason to improvise a home-use protocol.<Citation number={2} />

Cell cultures, animal models and clinical devices used for specific medical indications do not independently establish recommendations for consumer masks. For example, an oncology position paper concerns supportive-care questions in its own clinical setting; it is not a cosmetic face-mask treatment guide.<Citation number={3} />

### Why measurement methods matter

An irradiance figure becomes more useful when the manufacturer explains:

- The instrument used and its calibration.
- The measurement distance and surface.
- Whether the mask was flat or in its intended curved configuration.
- Which facial locations were measured.
- Whether the reported value is a spatial average, maximum or minimum.
- Whether the device was operating continuously or in a pulsed mode.

A maximum reading is not automatically the exposure across the entire face. Hadis and colleagues' radiometry paper is relevant background for these measurement questions; no model-specific measurement is supplied here.<Citation number={1} />

## Why Manufacturer Numbers Need Context

A specification sheet is useful, but it is not necessarily an independent measurement report. Ask how the number was obtained and whether the stated fluence was measured or calculated from irradiance and time.

A model-specific regulatory summary is another source to inspect for reported technical information. The FDA record in the background reading concerns one device, not a universal performance benchmark. This article does not quote or independently validate that model's optical output or regulatory status.<Citation number={5} />

Regulatory clearance or a substantial-equivalence determination should not be interpreted as proof that all devices with similar numbers have identical clinical outcomes.

Reporting guidance by Jenkins and Carroll addresses dose and beam parameters in clinical and laboratory studies. It is useful context for asking for a complete exposure description rather than one headline irradiance or fluence figure; it is not a consumer treatment recommendation.<Citation number={6} />

## Can You Calculate Fluence Yourself?

Sometimes. You need a reliable irradiance measurement at the relevant treatment plane, the exposure time and an appropriate description of how output varies.

For another **hypothetical mathematical example**:

- Irradiance: **8 mW/cm²**.
- Exposure duration: **500 seconds**.
- Calculation: **8 × 500 ÷ 1,000 = 4 J/cm²**.

That result describes calculated incident radiant exposure under the stated assumptions. It is **not a clinically validated treatment dose**.

The limitations matter:

1. The simple multiplication assumes constant irradiance; varying output needs an appropriate average or time integral.
2. The measurement must represent the actual treatment surface and operating configuration.
3. Spatially uneven illumination cannot be described fully by one reading.
4. Incident radiant exposure is not energy absorbed by a particular tissue target.
5. A correct calculation cannot, by itself, establish effectiveness or safety.

Do not extend a session beyond the model's instructions to reach a number taken from another device or an experimental paper.

## Comparing Devices Without Ranking by One Number

Ask whether the wavelengths, measurement planes, operating modes and exposure durations are comparable. Look for illumination uniformity, relevant human evidence and model-specific safety instructions alongside the arithmetic.

A higher irradiance is not automatically better. A higher fluence is not automatically more effective. A larger total optical output does not independently establish uniform exposure across the face.
